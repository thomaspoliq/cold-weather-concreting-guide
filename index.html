Cold Weather Concreting Guide

Concreting in Cold Weather: A Small Jobs Guide

Winter does not have to stop small concreting jobs, but it does change the rules. Cold slows the chemical reaction that gives concrete its strength, and a single frosty night can permanently damage concrete that was placed only hours earlier. With the right preparation, small slabs, paths and bases can still be laid well in the colder months.

The short answer: Never place concrete on frozen ground, and avoid placing it when the air temperature is around 5 degrees C and falling. Keep fresh concrete at or above about 5 degrees C, use warm mixing water, thaw and insulate the ground beforehand, then cover the finished work with insulated blankets and cure it for longer than you would in summer. If a hard frost is forecast for the first night and you cannot protect the work, postpone the job.

The temperature rules that matter

Standard UK practice, reflected in specifications such as BS 8500, works on a simple threshold: concrete should arrive and be placed at no less than 5 degrees C, and it should not be placed when the air temperature is falling through that mark with frost expected. What matters most is not the temperature at midday but the coldest temperature the young concrete will meet in its first 24 to 48 hours, because that is when it is at its weakest.

If fresh concrete freezes before it has gained some early strength, commonly taken as around 5 N/mm2, the water inside it expands as it turns to ice and breaks up the forming structure. That damage cannot be undone by later warm weather. Concrete that escapes freezing but stays cold is not ruined; it simply gains strength slowly, and given time and protection it can still reach its intended strength.

Preparing the ground and materials

Start a day or two early. Cover the area you will concrete with old blankets, straw or insulation boards to stop the frost getting in, or to thaw ground that is already frozen. Never be tempted to pour onto frozen subgrade and hope for the best: when that ground thaws in spring it softens and settles, and the slab cracks over the voids. Check with a spade on the morning of the pour; if the surface is still hard with ice, wait.

Store bagged cement and dry mix under cover and off the cold ground, and keep aggregates free of ice and snow. Warm mixing water is the simplest way to lift the temperature of a small batch: use water that feels comfortably warm to the hand, up to roughly 60 degrees C, and mix it with the aggregates before the cement goes in. Never pour boiling water directly onto cement, because the shock can cause the mix to stiffen almost instantly, a problem known as flash set.

Quantities still need planning in winter, because short daylight and slow finishing leave no time for a second trip to the merchants. For a shed base 3 m by 3 m at 100 mm thick you need 0.9 cubic metres of concrete. Running the figures through a slab concrete calculator before you order confirms the volume in seconds, and at that size a ready mix delivery, placed and covered in one short session, is usually a better winter choice than dozens of hand mixed batches.

Step by step on the day

  1. Check the forecast and the ground. Confirm no frozen subgrade and no severe frost warning for the first night that you cannot protect against.
  2. Work at the warmest time. Aim to place late morning so the concrete gets the mildest hours for its first set.
  3. Mix warm and place promptly. Use warm water, keep batches moving, and do not add extra water to improve workability.
  4. Compact and finish without delay. Cold slows finishing as well as setting, so have screeds, floats and edgers ready before the first batch lands.
  5. Do not overwork bleeding water. Bleed water rises slowly in the cold; trowelling it back in weakens the surface, so wait for it to disappear naturally.
  6. Cover immediately after finishing. Lay polythene to hold in moisture, then insulated blankets over the top, weighted at the edges against wind.
  7. Leave protection in place. Keep the covers on through the cold nights that follow, checking daily that they have not blown off or soaked through.

Whatever you choose, the principle is the same: trap the heat the cement generates as it hydrates, and keep frost off the surface until the concrete can look after itself.

Curing for longer, and a winter timeline

Cold concrete is slow concrete. Boards and formwork that could come off after a day in summer should stay on longer, foot traffic should wait, and the damp curing period should be extended. As a rough pattern, curing that takes three days in mild weather may need a week or more when temperatures hover just above freezing.

StageWinter guidance
Day 0Place in late morning, finish, cover with polythene and insulation straight away
Days 1 to 2Keep fully covered; no foot traffic; check covers after wind
Days 3 to 7Keep frost protection on overnight; light use only if the surface feels hard
After day 7Remove covers in a mild spell, not on a frosty morning; continue gentle use while strength builds

Frequently asked questions

Can I lay concrete at 4 degrees C?

It is risky for a small unheated job. Below about 5 degrees C the setting reaction slows dramatically and any overnight frost can damage the surface before strength develops. Unless you can warm the materials, place at the warmest time and insulate heavily, the sensible answer is to wait for a milder spell.

What happens if frost hits on the first night?

If the concrete had already stiffened and gained some strength, the damage may be limited to a weakened, dusty surface that scales later. If it froze while still soft, the slab is likely to be weak throughout and may need breaking out. Prevention, in the form of insulation, is far cheaper than either outcome.

Can I add antifreeze or an accelerator instead?

There is no antifreeze for concrete in the car sense. Accelerator admixtures do exist and shorten setting time, but chloride based accelerators should never be used in reinforced concrete because chlorides promote corrosion of the steel. Non chloride accelerators are available; follow the manufacturer's dosing exactly if you use one.

Does hot water harm the mix?

Warm water is good practice in winter; boiling water is not. Water up to about 60 degrees C, blended with the aggregates before the cement is added, raises the batch temperature safely. Boiling water meeting dry cement can trigger flash set and ruin the batch.

Will a slab poured in winter ever be as strong?

Yes, provided it never froze while young and was kept protected and curing. Cold slows the journey, not the destination. Concrete placed and protected properly in winter routinely reaches its design strength, it just takes longer to get there.

When can I take the blankets off?

Once the concrete has had several days of protection and the forecast shows nights staying above freezing, the covers can come off during a mild part of the day. Lifting insulation on a bitter morning can chill the surface suddenly, so pick your moment.

Conclusion

Cold weather concreting succeeds on preparation rather than bravery. Respect the 5 degree threshold, thaw and insulate the ground, mix with warm water, place at the best time of day, and then defend the young concrete with insulation while it cures slowly. Do those things and small winter jobs turn out every bit as sound as summer ones; skip them, and one frosty night can undo a full day of work.
